Balinese silver chains

Tiny spheres of silver, fused one at a time. The oldest decorative technique in jewelry, still alive in one Balinese village.

When jewelers say a chain is “Balinese” they usually mean one thing: the surface is worked with granulation, small beads of silver set into patterns and fused to the link, and often with hand-stamped motifs between them. The effect is texture you can feel with a fingertip, the opposite of a smooth machine chain.

Where it comes from

Granulation is ancient; Etruscan goldsmiths mastered it more than 2,500 years ago. In Bali the craft is centred on Celuk, a village in Gianyar regency a short drive from Ubud, where families of silversmiths have worked for generations. Traditional Balinese work uses granulation and fine wire to build motifs on beads, caps and links.

How to recognise handwork

  • The granules are slightly irregular in size and spacing. Cast imitations are perfectly even.
  • Under a loupe, the joint between granule and base is clean, not a blurred casting line.
  • Handwork links vary a little from one to the next. That variation is the point.

Why we darken it

Granulation only reads properly when the hollows are dark and the beads are bright. That is why we oxidize every Balinese section and rub back the high points by hand. In bright polished silver, granulation turns into glare.
