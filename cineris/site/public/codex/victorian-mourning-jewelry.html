Victorian mourning jewelry

For forty years a queen wore her husband’s hair. Half of Britain followed her.

Prince Albert died at Windsor in December 1861. Queen Victoria wore black for the rest of her life, until 1901, and a great deal of her jewelry held his hair or his portrait: lockets, bracelets, brooches. The court followed her, and then the country. For a generation, mourning jewelry was one of the largest branches of the trade.

The materials

  • Whitby jet, a fossilised wood from the Yorkshire coast, polished to a deep black.
  • Black enamel over gold, often with the name and dates of the dead.
  • Hair, woven, coiled or set under crystal.
  • Later, cheaper substitutes: vulcanite, bog oak, French jet (black glass).

Why it was not morbid

To the Victorians, keeping a piece of someone was tenderness, not horror. Photography was new and expensive; hair was the most personal relic available. A locket was something you could touch without anyone knowing what it meant.

What we borrow

We keep the tenderness and drop the requirement that someone be dead. Our hair link holds hair from the living as often as from the lost.
