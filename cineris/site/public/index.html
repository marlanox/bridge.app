Our chains are the whole sentence.

Ten centimetres of plain cable. Then a cameo the size of a fingernail. Then Balinese links stamped by hand. Then one ring of gold, the only warm thing in the chain.

Worn on black cashmere, by men and women who do not explain themselves.

Anatomy of a chain

Read it from the clasp.

0 cm · The clasp

A box clasp you can hear.

Solid, cast and hand-chased, with the house mark under the tongue. It closes with a click you feel in the collarbone.

3–13 cm · Plain cable

Silence first.

Forged oval links from 4.2 mm wire. Every chain opens with plain links, so that what follows reads as an event.

13 cm · The cameo

A face the size of a fingernail.

Carved shell, 14 mm, set in a granulated bezel. She looks away from the wearer, toward whoever is looking.

16–23 cm · Balinese links

Granulation, the old way.

Links stamped and beaded by hand in the manner of the silversmiths of Celuk, then blackened and rubbed back so the high points catch light like wet stone.

24 cm · 18k gold

One warm link.

A single ring of solid 18 karat gold. Only one. It is where you put your thumb when you are nervous.

26–31 cm · Byzantine weave

Weight.

The densest section. It turns the chain from jewelry into something with mass, so it falls straight on a sweater.

32 cm · The reliquary

A tear, kept.

A sealed glass link holding a lock of hair, a tear taken on silk, a line of a letter, or a pinch of earth from a place. We seal it at the bench while you watch.

Back of the neck · Hallmark & passport

Proof, and a door.

The 925 tag and the passport plate. The engraved code is the key to the chain’s cell in the Vault.

The Burning · No. I · 10 chains

Something is destroyed so that ten chains can exist.

For each numbered edition the house acquires one irreplaceable object and burns it in front of witnesses. Its ash is sealed into a single link in each of 10 chains. Then the edition closes forever.

Burning No. I is The Unopened Note: a folded note found inside a Victorian mourning locket, marked not to be opened until I am forgiven. We never opened it.

The reliquary link

Queen Victoria wore Albert’s hair for forty years.

When Prince Albert died in December 1861 the Queen went into black and stayed there. She wore lockets and bracelets holding his hair until her own death in 1901, and half of Britain copied her. Mourning jewelry was not morbid. It was a way to keep someone close enough to touch.

We revive it for the living as well as the dead. Bring a lock of hair, a line from a letter, earth from a place, or a tear. A tear does not keep, so we take it on a thread of silk, let it dry, and seal the silk in glass. It is the only thing on earth that came from that exact moment.

Present your chain

Every chain is born with a sealed cell in the Vault. The giver places a message inside (words, a photograph, a film, a proposal) and chooses the day it opens. Until then nobody can read it. Not the wearer. Not us.

Open the Vault

The Vault

A message that waits.

A father seals a letter for his daughter that opens on her thirtieth birthday. A man seals a proposal that opens in ninety days, and gives the chain today. Someone seals an accusation. Someone seals a confession to themselves.

When the wearer presents the passport code, the Vault answers with one sentence: this cell opens in 94 days. That is all. The waiting is part of the gift.

The Order

You do not choose your role.

When your chain is bound you draw a sealed card. It may say Sovereign. It may say Ever-Guilty. Each role carries one mission, entirely voluntary and never sold. Some roles are called, now and then, to a closed supper in Los Angeles.

I

The Witness

You saw something and said nothing. The Order asks you to say it once, to the right person.

II

The Ever-Guilty

The role nobody wants and everybody remembers. You carry blame that is not yours, gracefully.

III

The Keeper

You hold a sealed cell for another member. You will never read it. You will know when to hand it over.

IV

The Heir

Something will come to you from a member you have never met. Accept it without asking why.

V

The Messenger

You carry a letter across the city by hand. You may not mail it, text it or read it.

VI

The Debtor

You owe the world something unspecified. You pay it quietly.

VII

The Confessor

At a closed supper, one guest will tell you something true. You take it to the grave.

VIII

The Saint of Small Things

You work in the margins: children, animals, the forgotten.

The Concordance

Two chains make a key that neither has alone.

Put your passport code beside a friend’s, a lover’s, a brother’s. Together they produce a long key that exists only for that pair. It opens a cell in the Vault that neither of you could open alone: a prediction, an instruction, sometimes an invitation.

What it costs

Silver is priced by weight. Ours is priced by the hour.

Every chain is assembled by hand at the bench on Melrose, for one person. Most take nine to twenty-one days. Because each one is made for one neck and one story, nothing is returned or exchanged.
