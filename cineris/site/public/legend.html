The legend, as it is told at the bench

The note that was never opened.

Every house has a story it tells about itself. This is ours. We tell it the same way every time.

On Sunday mornings the parking lot of a high school on Fairfax turns into a market. Leather jackets, dead people’s china, records nobody wants. Somewhere between a stall of military buttons and a box of keys without locks, the founder of this house bought a Victorian mourning locket for eleven dollars.

It was black enamel over gold, worn thin at the edge where a thumb had rubbed it for decades. Inside, behind a crystal, was a coil of brown hair. Behind the hair, folded to the size of a postage stamp, was a note. On the outside of the note, in brown ink, one line:

Not to be opened until I am forgiven.

Nobody knows who wrote it, who was meant to forgive, or whether they ever did. The founder carried the locket for a year without opening the note. People asked about it. Everyone, without exception, wanted it opened. Nobody, without exception, wanted to be the one to do it.

The first Burning

At the end of that year the founder did the only thing that kept the promise and ended the temptation. The note was burned, unread, in a silver dish, in front of nine witnesses. The ash was divided into ten parts and sealed into ten links of glass and blackened silver. Ten chains were built around them. Those were the first chains of the Order.

What was written on the note is now inside ten people, scattered across Los Angeles, none of whom will ever know what it said. That is the whole idea of the house. Some things are worth more sealed.

Why silver, and why dark

Polished silver shines like a surgical instrument. We wanted the opposite: metal that looks like it has been held. Every chain is oxidized black and then rubbed back by hand so that the high points glow and the hollows stay dark, the way old church silver looks under candlelight. It is massive, it is quiet, and it gets better as it wears.

Why chains, and no pendants

A pendant is a full stop. Our chains are sentences: plain links, then an event, then texture, then a single ring of gold, then weight. You read them with your fingers. Men wear them over black cashmere. Women wear them doubled. Nobody asks what they mean, because it is obvious that you would not say.

The Order

The people who wear the first ten chains began, without being asked, to recognise one another. That is how the Order started: not as a club anyone joined, but as a fact. Today each new chain is bound with a passport, a sealed cell in the Vault, and a role drawn from a deck of twelve. There are suppers. There are missions. Most of it is never posted anywhere.

If you are reading this, you have been told about us by someone. That is the only way in.
